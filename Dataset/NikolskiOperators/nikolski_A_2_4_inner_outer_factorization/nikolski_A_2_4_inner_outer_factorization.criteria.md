# Criteria: nikolski_A_2_4_inner_outer_factorization

**Statement:** [nikolski_A_2_4_inner_outer_factorization.md](nikolski_A_2_4_inner_outer_factorization.md) · **Lean:** [nikolski_A_2_4_inner_outer_factorization.lean](nikolski_A_2_4_inner_outer_factorization.lean) · **Context:** [nikolski_A_2_4_inner_outer_factorization.context.md](nikolski_A_2_4_inner_outer_factorization.context.md)

## What the theorem says

Every function $f$ in the Hardy space $H^2$ on the unit disc, other than the zero function, splits
as a product $f = f_{\mathrm{inn}} f_{\mathrm{out}}$. The first factor is *inner*: bounded and
analytic on the disc, with boundary values of modulus $1$ almost everywhere. The second factor is
*outer*, meaning that the polynomial multiples of it fill up $H^2$; equivalently it is given by the
exponential of a Poisson-type integral of $\log\lvert f_{\mathrm{out}}\rvert$. The splitting is
unique up to a constant of modulus $1$, which moves from one factor to the other. Smirnov also
records that the shift-invariant subspace generated by $f$ is the one generated by
$f_{\mathrm{inn}}$.

| # | Requirement | Does the ground truth have it? |

| 1 | $f$ lies in $H^2$: analytic on the open unit disc, with the $L^2$ norms on the circles of radius $r < 1$ bounded by one finite constant. | ✅ `hf : HardyClass 2 f`, whose boundedness clause is `∃ C : ℝ≥0∞, C < ∞ ∧ ∀ r ∈ [0,1), eLpNorm … ≤ C`. |

| 5 | The product identity holds on the disc. | ✅ `∀ z ∈ Metric.ball (0 : ℂ) 1, f z = θ z * g z`. |

## Mistakes to check for

Each row is an error we expect models to make. A formalization that makes any of these is
wrong, even if it compiles.

| # | Mistake | Why it is wrong |
|---|---------|-----------------|
| 1 | Writing "$f \ne 0$" as an inequality of total functions `ℂ → ℂ`. | A function that is zero on the whole disc and nonzero outside it satisfies that hypothesis. Outer functions never vanish on the disc, so no factorization exists and the statement becomes false. |
| 2 | Asking for `OuterFunction ⊤ g`, i.e. a bounded outer factor. | The outer part of an $H^2$ function need not be bounded. The exponent has to travel with the outer factor. |
| 3 | Splitting the uniqueness into two independent existentials, or asserting only $\theta' = c\theta$. | Then the two constants need not be inverse to each other, and the product $\theta' g'$ need not equal $f$. The compensation is the content of "up to a constant factor". |
| 4 | Writing `∀ z, ∃ c, …` instead of `∃ c, ∀ z, …`. | A different constant at each point is a far weaker claim; it does not say the two factorizations differ by a rotation. |
| 5 | Asserting $f = \theta g$ for every $z \in \mathbb{C}$. | The functions are total but only their restriction to the disc is constrained by the Hardy hypotheses, so the identity off the disc is neither asserted by the book nor provable. |
| 6 | Defining "outer" as "has no zeros in the disc". | Not equivalent. The singular inner function $\exp\!\big(\tfrac{z+1}{z-1}\big)$ has no zeros in the disc and is inner, not outer. |
| 7 | Dropping the `HasRadialBoundaryValues` conjunct from "inner" and asserting only that the boundary modulus is $1$. | `boundaryValue` is defined as `limUnder`, which returns an arbitrary value wherever the radial limit does not exist. Without the existence clause, the modulus condition is a statement about junk. |

## Notes on the ground truth

- The book defines "outer" by the subspace property $E_g = H^2$. The ground truth substitutes the
  exponential Poisson representation
  $g(z) = c\exp\!\big(\tfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\tfrac{\zeta_t+z}{\zeta_t-z}\log\lvert g(\zeta_t)\rvert\,dt\big)$
  with $\lvert c\rvert = 1$. The two agree for $g \in H^p$, and the integral form is the workable
  one, but a candidate that uses the book's subspace definition should be scored at least as well.
  The $1/(2\pi)$ against `volume` on `Ioc 0 (2π)` is the correct normalization.
- The exponent is a Bochner integral, and Lean gives a non-integrable Bochner integral the value
  $0$. If that branch were taken, the formula would read $g \equiv c$ with $\lvert c\rvert = 1$; but
  then $\log\lvert g\rvert = 0$ almost everywhere, which *is* integrable, and the formula holds
  honestly. So the degenerate branch defeats itself rather than opening a loophole. For a fixed $z$
  in the open disc the Poisson factor is bounded, so integrability of the product is exactly
  integrability of $\log\lvert g\rvert$.
- Because everything is guarded by `∀ z ∈ Metric.ball (0 : ℂ) 1`, the uniqueness asserted is
  uniqueness on the disc only. That is unavoidable when analytic functions are modelled as total
  functions `ℂ → ℂ`.
- `HardyClass` encodes "$\sup_{0 \le r < 1}$" as "there is a finite $C$ bounding every radial
  norm"; the `C < ∞` conjunct is what makes this a real boundedness claim rather than the trivial
  `≤ ⊤`. Writing `⨆ r, eLpNorm … < ∞` would be closer to the printed $\sup_r$.
- The missing third assertion (Requirement 8) is the honest gap in this file.

### Fatal — any of these caps the total at 25

- Requirement 7 with the two uniqueness relations carrying independent constants.

- Requirement 8 omitted, dropping the identification $E_f = f_{\mathrm{inn}}H^2$.

### Domain-specific pitfalls for this problem

- "$f \ne 0$" means $f$ is not the zero element, not that $f$ has no zeros.
- Inner is $|\theta^*| = 1$ a.e. on the circle; outer is a statement about the shift-invariant subspace generated by the function.
- The factorization identity holds on the disc; the inner condition holds a.e. on the circle. Both venues appear.
- Uniqueness is up to a single unimodular constant, shared between the two factors.
